Problemata, Book 13 by Aristotle, in the original Greek (Bekker (Berlin, 1831)), with the English translation of E. S. Forster. Nearly nine hundred questions of natural science, each asked as “Why…?” and answered with a conjecture, gathered into thirty-eight books.

1For this reason the perspiration of some people is sometimes unpleasant, particularly in those whose perspiration is not usually unpleasant, as the result of disease. Also the exhalations and eructations of those who are in an unconcocted state are unpleasant. The same cause must be ascribed for evil odour in the flesh and in that which is analogous to it (by 5which I mean that which in other animals corresponds to flesh); for here too there is sometimes unconcocted excretion. This does not happen in plants, because they contain no excretions.

1There are some such perfumes, just as there are some flowers from which scents are made, which deteriorate when rubbed or heated or dried, white violets, for example; but others remain the same, for instance roses. The unguents too made from flowers of the former class change, while those made from the latter do not; and so rose-perfume is least liable to change. Also unguents have a more unpleasant 5odour on those whose perspiration is malodorous, through mingling with their opposite, just as honey when mixed with salt becomes not sweeter but less sweet.
