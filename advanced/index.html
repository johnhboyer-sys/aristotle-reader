How search works

Tools for asking questions of Aristotle's Greek, and of its translations, that a plain word search cannot answer. You need no Greek for most of them, and you can read the sections in any order. Every number on this page is counted from the texts this site serves: 47 works, 995,731 Greek words. Check any of them against the search itself.

First, three things worth knowing

Greek changes the ends of words. English mostly does not: "virtue" looks the same wherever it stands. Greek changes the ending to show the word's job, so ἀρετή, ἀρετῆς, and ἀρετήν are one word doing three jobs. A search for one spelling would miss most of the places the word occurs. That is why Form starts at Any form of this word: type any form, and the search finds them all.

The search reads every work. In starts at All works: all 47, including the 3 dubious and 13 spurious works that came down under Aristotle's name, and Porphyry's Isagoge. Set In to Authentic works for Aristotle's 30, or Choose works… to pick your own.

You can type Greek without a Greek keyboard. Type texnh and the search reads τέχνη. Typing Greek has the letters.

Numbers like 1094a15 are Bekker numbers, the standard way of citing Aristotle: page, column, and line of Bekker's Berlin edition. Most editions and translations print them in the margin, so the same number finds the same passage in any of them. Results here use them throughout.

ΑTyping Greek

Use this when you have no Greek keyboard.

Beta Code writes Greek in Latin letters. Each Greek letter has one Latin letter, and the marks over the letters are written with ) ( / \ = | + after the letter they belong to. Scholars have long used it to put Greek into computers, and the search box reads it without being asked: a)reth/ is ἀρετή, and texnh is τέχνη.

A few letters catch people out. h is η and w is ω, so e and o stay short. q is θ, c is ξ, and x is χ. A final s becomes ς by itself.

Turn on Beta Code, beside the search box, and the letters turn Greek as you type: a)reth/ becomes ἀρετή in the box, so you can see what you asked for. With it off, the box keeps your Latin letters and the search reads them the same way.

You may leave the marks off: the search ignores accents, breathings, and capitals. Turn on Accents to match the marks exactly. Then λόγος and λογός are different searches, and a word typed without marks finds only words printed without them.

The keyboard

Keyboard, beside the search box, opens a Greek keyboard on the screen, laid out as Apple lays out Greek. The marks have a row of their own: type a vowel, then press a mark, and it joins the vowel. Press another mark of the same kind and it replaces the first.

ΒWildcards

Use this when you know how a word begins but not how it ends.

End a word with * and it stands for whatever follows. With Form set to Only as I typed it, fron* reaches 60 different spellings across the works: φρόνησις, φρόνιμος, φρονεῖν, and the rest of that family at once. Since Greek marks a word's job at the end, one wildcard gathers the whole paradigm. Under Any form of this word, the search goes further and adds every other form of the words those spellings belong to.

A wildcard reaches further the more text you give it. In the Nicomachean Ethics alone, l* reaches 191 different spellings; across all 47 works it reaches 1,265, out of 55,317 in the corpus. The search counts places, not spellings, so it answers l* with passages, not with a word count.

A star can also stand inside a word, and ? stands for exactly one letter. l*s is every word that begins with λ and ends with ς, 358 different spellings. log?s finds λόγος but not λόγους, which has two letters in that place. The pattern must match the whole word, so a star in the middle never quietly becomes "every word beginning λ".

Greek also takes . for one letter, + for one or more of the letter before, [ ] for one of several letters ([^ ] for any letter but those), ( | ) for either of two, and ^ and $ for the start and end of the word. (φιλ|σοφ)ια finds both φιλία and σοφία.

A star at the start of a word depends on how you type it. In Greek letters it is a wildcard: *ικος finds every word ending in -ικος. In Beta Code a star before a letter makes a capital, so there you write the ending as .*ikos$. Beta Code also reads ( ) | + as marks, so groups and alternatives need Greek letters. In English a leading star is always a wildcard: *ness finds happiness and goodness.

Running text

Set Form to Running text and the search reads the Greek as one stream of letters, across the breaks between words. λογ finds λόγος and ἀναλογία: the letters wherever they stand, at the start of a word or inside one. Here . is one letter, .* any run of letters, and | either of two. Each break between words counts as one space, so you can search across words too. Turn on Punctuation to match the stops as well: , . ; and ·.

ΓLemma

Use this when you want one dictionary word, in every form, and nothing else.

Any form of this word already works out the dictionary word from whatever form you type. Lemma is for when you would rather choose it yourself. Turn on Lemma and the box asks for a dictionary word. Start typing, and a list of Dictionary words opens: the headword, a short meaning, and how often it occurs. Pick the one you mean. The search then finds every form of that word and no other: no spelling that merely looks like it, and no other word that one of its forms could also belong to.

Typing ψυχ offers ψυχή first, as the commonest, then the other words that begin that way, such as ψυχρός, so you choose rather than guess. The list holds 16,571 entries, one for every word the search can find. A term set to any form in Proximity picks from the same list.

The search ignores accents, so words that differ only by an accent or breathing, such as ὅρος ("boundary"), ὄρος ("mountain"), and ὀρός ("whey"), are one entry in the list, shown together. No search here can separate them.

ΔWord endings

Use this when you know a phrase but want every form of it, not just the one you typed.

An exact phrase search finds only the version you typed. Search two or more Greek words and a button appears beside the results: Find this phrase in any inflection. It works out which dictionary word each of your words belongs to, then looks for every form of those words standing in the order you gave.

The phrase index shows how much that gathers. τὸ καλόν ("the fine") stands 94 times as those exact letters, in 15 works. Read as dictionary words, ὁ καλός, which gathers τοῦ καλοῦ, τῷ καλῷ, τῶν καλῶν, and the rest, it stands 303 times, in 23. To find those yourself you would have to know that τό, τοῦ, and τῷ are one word. The tool knows it for you.

It invents nothing. ὡς ἐπὶ τὸ πολύ ("for the most part") stands 239 times as written and 239 times read as dictionary words. A tool that always found more would tell you nothing.

ΕEnglish searches

Use this to find a word in the translations, in any of its forms.

Choose English beside the search box. An English search finds a word in all its forms by default: a plural or possessive finds its singular, and back. In the main translation of each work "soul" stands 998 times, and "souls" and "soul's" 32 times more. Form set to Any form of this word finds them all; Only as I typed it finds the one spelling you gave. Irregular plurals are known too: "man" finds "men".

Match works as it does for Greek. All words wants every word somewhere in the passage, Any word at least one of them, and Exact phrase the words in a row, in your order.

Every translation

11 works have more than one translation here, and an English search asks each of them. A passage that several translations match is one hit: the Greek once, and one translation's English. Under it stand the names of the translators whose words matched. Click a name to read that translation of the passage instead. The Translations list beside the results counts the hits in each, and choosing one shows only its hits.

Greek and English at once

Choose Both and you get two boxes, Greek and English. Hits match decides whether a passage must match both languages or either language.

ϚSearch by grammar

Use this to find a kind of word rather than a particular word.

Every Greek word here carries a grammatical description: its case, number, tense, and so on. You can search those descriptions instead of the spelling. In Proximity, set a term's Kind to by grammar, and the choices open in rows: Case, Number, Gender, Tense, Mood, Voice, Person, and Degree. Pick a value in as many rows as you like; a word must match them all. You need not know how any of these forms are spelled.

Grammar searched across all of Aristotle would not be a question: there are 38,688 feminine genitive plurals in the works, and a list of them tells you about Greek rather than about Aristotle. So a grammar term runs on its own only once something narrows it: fewer works under In, or a word near or not near it. Ask without either, and the page offers Choose works and Add a word near or not near instead of results. Aorist passive participles in the De Anima is a question; a word near a verb in the optative is another.

Grammar also sorts results you already have. Set Group by to grammar and the hits fall into groups by their analysis, with a Grammar list beside them: tick a case or a tense there to keep only those hits.

When the letters could be two forms

The same Greek letters can be two different forms. When that happens the hit shows what is left open, rather than choosing for you.

Aristotle's works hold 6,280 words that can be read as optatives. Of those, 2,467 have only that reading; a hit on any of the rest comes back marked, because you could read those letters another way. The mark is no flaw in the search. It reports a fact about the Greek.

A clearer case: of the 440 genitive duals (the dual is the form for pairs), not one is certain. In the dual, genitive and dative share one ending. The search cannot tell them apart because Greek does not, and it says so.

ΖProximity

Use this to find where two ideas are discussed together.

Proximity finds passages where two or more terms stand close together. Press Proximity, beside Search, to open the editor. Search either word alone and you get every passage that holds it; ask for both together and you get the passages where Aristotle is doing something with the pair. ψυχή near ἀρετή in the Nicomachean Ethics is one such question.

The editor

  • Kind: what a term is. any form is a dictionary word in every form, picked from the list (see Lemma); as typed is one spelling; exact phrase is several words in a row; by grammar is a description (see Search by grammar).
  • Distance, on Term 1's row: how close is close. Within 5 words at first; you can set it as high as 50, or choose the same line of Greek (the same pair, in one line).
  • Relation, on every term after the first: near, before, after, or not near, and the term it is measured from. Usually that is Term 1, but it can be any earlier term.
  • and or or, before each term after the first. A term joined by or stands in for the one before it, and either will do: either ἡδονή or λύπη near ἀρετή. It takes that term's relation, and its row says so.
  • Add term and Remove. A search holds up to four terms.
  • In this order: every term must appear in the order you gave. Once any term says before or after, those settle the order, and the line reads Order set by before or after.
  • Keep hits that cross a chapter: on at first, since an argument often runs through a chapter break. Untick it to keep each match inside one chapter.

Each term must fall within the distance of the term it is measured from. The distance is counted between those two words alone, not across the whole group, so three terms within 10 words can span more than 10 words in all. Not near turns that round: the passage must have no such word within the distance of its term. ἡδονή before ἀρετή, with λύπη not near asks for pleasure without pain.

Once a proximity search has run, it reads as one sentence above the editor, with Turn off, Close, and Guide under it (Change in place of Close when the editor is shut). A change in the editor is not searched until you press its Search. Until then the sentence is marked Last search, and Not searched yet beside the button shows the search you are building.

A match never crosses from one book into the next, however wide the distance: the last words of one book and the first of the next are not near each other in any useful sense.

ΗReading the results

Use this to make a long list of hits answer your question.

The count is of instances: each place the search matched. Each instance shows its Bekker number, the Greek with the match marked, and the English under it. Click a Greek word in a hit to see its dictionary entry: the headword, its meaning, how the form parses, Show LSJ definition for the full entry, and how often it appears across Aristotle.

  • Group by: by work, with a heading for each; by form, each spelling apart; or by grammar.
  • Sort: Bekker order, Works A–Z, Forms A–Z, or Commonest form first. A sort orders the page you are on; it never moves a hit to another page.
  • Context: 1 line, 3 lines, or the Whole section each hit stands in.
  • Per page: 20, 40, or 100 hits, or Citations only, the places without the text.

A page never splits a chapter. Each chapter's hits sit under a line that names it, "Chapter 13" and its title where the text has one, so a page that ends short ends at a chapter.

Beside the results, Works with hits counts the hits in each work, most first. Choose one to see only its hits, and choose it again to see them all. Forms lists every spelling the search found, with its count: untick the ones you do not want and press Apply. Export results as CSV gives you the whole list as a spreadsheet, and Save search keeps the search in this browser.

A search is written into the address bar as you run it, so you can bookmark it, send it to someone, or step back through your searches with the browser's back button.

ΘThe phrase index

Use this when you do not yet know what to look for.

Search answers "where does this occur?" The phrase index answers a different question: what does Aristotle repeat? It lists every phrase of two to five words that appears at least twice anywhere in the works: 204,515 of them as written, 462,768 when forms of the same word count together, and 389,447 in the English. (If you have met the term, these are what linguists call n-grams.)

Much of Aristotle's technical vocabulary is set phrases rather than single words, and no word search will show them to you: τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι ("essence") occurs 103 times across 8 works; ὡς ἐπὶ τὸ πολύ ("for the most part") 239 times; κατὰ συμβεβηκός ("accidentally") 337 times in 29. Not one of those is a word. Each is an idea, and browsing is the only way to meet them.

Reading a row

Every row is one phrase and four numbers. This is the row for τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι:

The same phrase, counted two ways

Count phrases by reads the same works three ways. Word as written counts the letters on the page: change one ending and it is a different row. Word in any of its forms counts the words behind the letters, so every form of a phrase gathers into one row. Either takes the phrase as it stands on the page: type τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι and the second finds it under ὁ τίς εἰμί εἰμί, where it is stored, and tells you it read the words that way. English translation does the same for the English.

The second row is never the smaller: it gathers the phrase's other endings in with it. It also asks more of you. The same dictionary words can make phrases that are not the one you wanted, and the index cannot tell the difference: εἰμί covers ἦν, ἐστιν, and ἔστω alike. Use it to find what you would otherwise miss, then read as written to see what you have.

The score changes between the two rows for what is, in the main, the same expression. Nothing has weakened. Distinctiveness measures surprise against the words a phrase is made of, and the two rows are made of different words: ὁ and εἰμί are commoner than the exact shapes τό and ἦν, so a phrase built from them surprises less.

Using the list

  • Sort starts at Distinctiveness, which asks whether a phrase occurs more often than its words would lead you to expect. Sort by Frequency instead and you see the grammar of Greek rather than the thought of Aristotle.
  • Length, Minimum count, and Work narrow the list, and Phrase starts with finds a phrase by its first words. Sorting never removes anything.
  • An English phrase that recurs across several works may be one translator's habit rather than Aristotle's, so read its Works column. An English phrase is a place to look, not evidence about the Greek.

Click any phrase to see every place it occurs, grouped by work, each linked to that passage in the reader. A way to use it: pick the work you are reading and a length of four or five, then sort by distinctiveness. You get that work's set expressions, often the working vocabulary of its argument, including phrases no glossary would have sent you to.

ΙWhat these tools cannot tell you

All of this rests on a word-by-word grammatical description of the Greek, and that description is often uncertain. Where it is, these tools say so rather than choose an answer and present it as fact. The limits worth knowing:

  • A hit marked with more than one reading comes from a form the description cannot settle. Of the 6,280 possible optatives, 2,467 are settled and the rest are not.
  • Searches ignore accents unless you turn on Accents. And of the 53,867 spellings in the works, 7,179 could belong to more than one dictionary word, so a search for one word in all its forms can bring back another word that shares a spelling.
  • The phrase index shows Greek without accents, because it does not record them. We do not guess them.
  • Later editors made the chapter divisions, not Aristotle, and in 24 of the 47 works we know where a chapter begins only to the Bekker line, not to the word. A search that keeps matches inside a chapter says so when one of those works is involved.
  • None of these tools reads Greek. They match forms and count them. What a passage means remains your work.

The grammatical descriptions come from Morpheus, by way of Diogenes; the dictionary is Liddell–Scott–Jones.
