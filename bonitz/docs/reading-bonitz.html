How to read an entry in Bonitz

An entry is three voices set solid in one column: Aristotle's Greek, Bonitz's Latin, and the citations that bind them. Learn to see the three and the density stops being density.

Worked on τὸ ἀγαθόν, one of the longest entries in the book — it runs most of a column before its second sense even opens.

The citation grammar

work · book · chapter · Bekker page, column, line

Ηα1. 1094a3
workbookchap. page col line

Read it right to left and it is unambiguous. 1094a3 is a Bekker address — page 1094, left column, line 3 — and Bekker's pages run in one unbroken series across the whole corpus, never repeating. So the page alone fixes the work. Everything to the left of it is Bonitz telling you where you are in Aristotle's own divisions: Η is the Nicomachean Ethics, α its first book, chapter 1.

That redundancy is the whole reason the text can be checked by machine, and the reason it can be read at speed. Two independent statements of the same fact, side by side: if they disagree, one of them is a misprint or a misreading.

Case is significant and is Bonitz's own. Ζι is the Historia animalium and ζ is περὶ Νεότητος; Μ is the Metaphysics and μ the Meteorologica; Π is the Politics and π the Problemata. Nothing in the layout warns you. There are 48 sigla and the key is on p. 14.

What Bonitz leaves out

the hardest convention in the book

A column of this density is bought by omission. Once a work is named, Bonitz stops naming it, and he drops it at two different depths — which is why the run of citations after the first lemma looks like noise until you know the rule.

So a bare Greek letter is a book of the work you are already in, and a bare number is a chapter of the book you are already in. The trap is that book letters and work sigla are drawn from the same alphabet. ζ1. 558b13 is not the work ζ; it is book 6 of whatever was last named — and the page settles it, because ζ occupies 467b–470b and 558 is nowhere near it.

Bonitz's Latin

the editorial voice — everything that is not Aristotle

The Greek is quoted; the Latin is Bonitz speaking. He uses it for three jobs: numbering the senses, describing what a passage does, and pointing sideways. It is compressed to the edge of legibility and never explained, because his reader had the Latin.

A cross-reference points into the index itself, not into Aristotle: cf φύσις 2 c. means see the entry φύσις, sense 2, subsection c — so the senses carry lettered subdivisions below the numbers, and the index is a graph, not a list.

Sorts you will not recognise

ligatures, numerals, and the fragments

SortIsNote
ȣουa true ligature, and it takes its own accents — ȣ͂ is οῦ
ϗκαίthe kai-compendium; ϗ̀ carries the grave it would have had
ϛ6stigma, in book numerals only — πκϛ is Problemata 26
ffragmentf195. 1513a24 — Bekker's fragment pages, past the end of the works

Book numerals are ordinary Greek alphabetic numbers, so a two-letter book is arithmetic: πκϛ is π (Problemata) plus κ 20 + ϛ 6. The Metaphysics is the exception that catches everyone — its book letters are names, not numbers, the tradition's own Α, α elatton, Β, Γ … Ν. That is why ΜΑ takes a capital where nothing else does, and why Μν is the fourteenth book and not the fiftieth.

Editors, not works

a siglum that follows the page number

In the specimen, ΜΑ3. 983a32 Bz. — Bz is Bonitz's own 1848 edition of the Metaphysics, cited for a reading at that line. Editor sigla always follow the Bekker number; work sigla always precede it, so position alone tells them apart.

Bk
Bekker — the Academy text itself; Bk2, Bk3 are his later editions of some books
Bz
Bonitz, Aristotelis Metaphysica, Berlin 1848
Did
the Didot Opera omnia, Paris 1848 ff.
A
Aubert & Wimmer — and it takes a work siglum: AΖι is Aubert on the Historia animalium

Bonitz closes the key with a sentence worth keeping: everything not abbreviated in that list is written out in full, so an unexplained compound is a compound you have misread.

Where the checking stops

what the page alone cannot settle

Because Bekker's pages are disjoint per work, a machine can confirm every citation whose work is named — and can recover the work for one whose siglum was dropped. That is how the corpus is validated: 4,886 citations, of which 26 do not resolve.

It stops one level short. The checker knows where a work runs, not where each of its books runs — and this very entry has two citations that name a book whose Bekker span does not contain the page:

Both pass every check, because 1031b and 1050a are inside the Metaphysics and that is all the machine knows. Whether these are Bonitz's slips, the compositor's, or our reading of the ink is a question only the scan can answer — and it is the next check to build.
