One document, folded

Round 1 put Aquinas in a panel beside Aristotle and kept the division in a separate outline. This round drops both. There is one document for the commentary, and Aquinas's division of the text is its shape:

  • His division is the rows. Each heading is a part he names, then Aristotle's book and chapter, then Aquinas's lectiones, as in Berquist's division of the Posterior Analytics: Aristotle's preface, I.1–2 (lect. 1–2). The leaves are numbered units of Aristotle's text, cut where he cuts it (the Dumb Ox numbering). In Outline the division stands alone; in Text, lectio starts run through it as thin rules.
  • Aquinas's own parts are not rows. The opening of lectio 1 (n. 1–2), where his preface runs straight into the division of the text, comments on no part of Aristotle's text. It follows the lectio rule under a bar marked Aquinas, and the rows after it are Aristotle's division alone. A paragraph that sets out how a part divides sits on that part's heading, beside the list of its parts: n. 10 on What enters the definition.
  • The five texts are columns. Greek, Smith's English, Moerbeke's Latin, Aquinas's Latin, an English of Aquinas. There is no English of Moerbeke: whoever reads his Latin reads Latin. Switch on any of them; each row lines them all up on the same unit.
  • Aristotle's three texts line up by sentence. They are Aristotle's Greek with Smith's English, and the Latin Aquinas read; Moerbeke's Greek is marks on Ross's. So the column is cut into sentences, one of Moerbeke's to a row with the Greek and Smith that answer it (17 rows in lectio 1; Smith is cut at a colon or semicolon where he runs two of Moerbeke's together). Adding a text adds lines, not columns. Smith sits under the Greek behind a rule, at full size. The study bar's Aristotle column switch compares four ways to show them: Blocks (as before), Sentences (every text under every sentence, Greek and Latin side by side from about 1800px), Sentence opens (one text runs on; the sentence Aquinas is on, or the one tapped, opens the rest), and Tabs (one at a time; a switch keeps the place).
  • The short text holds still. Inside a row, Aristotle's cell stays pinned while Aquinas scrolls past it, and it lights and scrolls to the words the paragraph you are reading is about. At the end of the row it lets go.
  • Divisions come from folding, not from a second view. Show the whole of De Anima, a book, or lectio 1, as headings only (the division) or with the text. Any heading folds.
  • Read Aquinas and Read Aristotle are the same grid. Read Aristotle folds Aquinas to a one-line gloss on each unit, so Aristotle's text runs on; open a gloss where you want him.
  • Moerbeke's Greek is an apparatus, not a seventh column. No one has identified the Greek manuscript Moerbeke translated, so his Greek can only be inferred from his Latin. The switch marks each place where his Latin implies a Greek other than Ross's, in both texts, with the same letter; a short apparatus under each unit gives Ross ] the implied reading, the Latin that implies it, a grade, and the Aquinas paragraph that leans on it. Ross / Moerbeke swaps the implied readings into the Greek, so you can read his text as reconstructed.

What it costs

  • In Read Aristotle the text comes cut into Aquinas's units, under his headings, not into the modern chapters. The site's chapter reader stays as it is for reading Aristotle without him.
  • A unit taller than the screen scrolls inside its own cell. Unit [4] (402a10–22) is the longest here; with Greek and Smith stacked it needs that.
  • Phones get one Aristotle text at a time, in a strip pinned above Aquinas; the strip expands on a tap.

Decisions

  1. Unit grain. One unit per leaf of his division (seven in lectio 1, as here), or one per "cum dicit «…»", which is finer and closer to Dumb Ox?
  2. Read Aristotle. Keep his headings between units, or show only unit numbers and rules so the text reads more nearly straight through?
  3. Front page. Is De Anima + Outline the commentary's works page, or does it still want a separate front page?
  4. Defaults. Which columns open by default at each width? Now: Greek, with Aquinas's Latin and English.

What is real

  • Greek and Smith's English are the site's build; Smith is split by line between the real Bekker ticks, so a unit's English edge can be a line off.
  • Aquinas's Latin is Corpus Thomisticum's (the Turin 1959 text). Moerbeke's is Parma vol. 20, hand-corrected from archive.org's OCR, unchecked against the scan.
  • The English of Aquinas is a draft written for this mockup.
  • The division of lectio 1 and the unit edges were mapped by hand. Book I below "The treatise" is read from each lectio's opening sentence in Parma and from the division he gives in I.6; Books II and III have only the two levels his Book II, lectio 1 states. Lectio starts are checked against the Greek and the Foster–Humphries ranges on isidore.co.
  • Moerbeke's Greek is my reading of his Latin against Ross, word by word, for lectio 1 only: eight places, one strong, one likely, six slight. A second model (GPT-6.1-Sol) checked it: it dropped two of my first entries (περί at 402a3; endelechia, a spelling Latin inherited) and added two (aliqua, et si). It is not checked against Ross's or Gauthier's apparatus, and the Latin is Parma's printing, not Gauthier's critical text. Some readings may come from James of Venice, whose version Moerbeke revised.
