Showing Aquinas beside Aristotle

A working mockup for one lectio: Aquinas on De Anima I, lectio 1, against 402a1–403a2. The three views above are the two ways in (Aquinas as the book, or Aristotle as the book) and the commentary's own contents page. Switch widths in the bar to see what each screen can hold. On the reading views, hover a lemma in Aquinas, click a Greek line, or click a line number.

What the numbers say

4.3×Aquinas's Latin against the Greek it comments on (90,795 words to 20,959)

~38Bekker lines per lectio; lectio 1 covers 54 (402a1–403a2)

So a screen of Aristotle carries about four screens of Aquinas. Two panes that scroll together cannot both be readable: one of them has to hold still while the other moves.

Three rules the mockup tests

  1. The short text follows the long one, never the reverse. When you read Aquinas, the Aristotle pane holds the lectio's passage and lights the lines the current paragraph is about; it moves a line or two per paragraph. When you read Aristotle, the Aquinas panel does not chase the text (it would jump four screens per screen). It marks which paragraphs cover the lines in view and offers to go there.
  2. Mark his structure, not his presence. Aquinas comments on every line, so a dot on every annotated line would mark the whole text. The margin shows where his lectiones start and where his division of the text turns. Any line number opens what he says about that line.
  3. Each pane keeps its own language switch, and a pane folds its second language before the panes fold. Aristotle: Greek, Moerbeke's Latin, English, or Greek with English. Aquinas: Latin, both, or English.

A fifth text

Aquinas did not read Greek. He comments on William of Moerbeke's Latin, and his lemmata quote it word for word. The Parma edition prints that Latin at the head of every lectio, along with a later Latin version (its "Recens"). Offering Moerbeke in the Aristotle pane lets a Latin reader match each quoted lemma exactly; the Greek still matches, but through a translation.

What is real in the mockup

  • Greek and J. A. Smith's English: the site's own build of De Anima. Smith's English is split by line from the real Bekker ticks (every fifth line) and spread between them by length, so line-level English is approximate.
  • Aquinas's Latin and Moerbeke's Latin: the Parma edition, vol. 20 (1866), public domain, from archive.org's OCR, corrected by hand for this mockup and not yet checked against the scan.
  • The English of Aquinas: a working draft written for this mockup. It is not a candidate for the site.
  • Which lines each paragraph expounds, and where each lemma falls in the Greek: mapped by hand for lectio 1.
  • Lectio start lines on the contents page: read by hand from Parma's Moerbeke incipits, unchecked; lectiones 7 and 8 were not read. Word counts per lectio are counts of the Latin.

Two corrections to the decision doc

  • Lectio 1 runs 402a1–403a2, not 402a1–a22. Its paragraphs 11–15 cover "Primum autem fortassis" through "dialectice dictae sunt et vanae omnes", and Parma prints Moerbeke's text for exactly that span.
  • Parma gives each lectio a one-sentence summary and prints two Latin versions of Aristotle before the commentary. The schema has no place for either yet.

Decisions for you

  1. Follow or wait. Reading Aristotle with the Aquinas panel open: should the panel stay put and mark where the text is (the default here), or follow the text when it comes to rest (the Follow switch)?
  2. Which pane gets its second language first at laptop width: Aristotle (Greek with English) or Aquinas (Latin with English)?
  3. Moerbeke's Latin as an Aristotle option in the Aquinas reader. It is a fifth text to OCR and correct, from the same scans.
  4. English for Aquinas. No public-domain English of this commentary is known (Foster and Humphries, 1951, is unresolved). Latin only, an AI translation like the Themistius plan, or wait? This decides whether "Both" exists on the public site.
  5. The citation unit. Marietti paragraph numbers (I, l. 1, n. 6) as Aquinas's addresses in URLs and citations.
