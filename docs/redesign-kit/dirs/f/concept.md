# F · Objects

**The idea.** Each work is shown the way a museum shows one object: cut
from its photograph, lit on a dark wall, with a label that says why it is
there. The site reads as the catalogue of an exhibition about Aristotle.

**Why it suits Aristotle.** He thought with things. His best examples survive
as real objects from his world: the bronze of a statue, wax and a seal ring, a
sea battle, the hand as a tool for tools. The label quotes the line that names
the thing, so each pairing explains itself.

**Pages.**
- Home: a cover. Aristotle’s portrait, larger than life, lit from above, his
  name in English and Greek, search on the wall. Below, every work as a
  checklist that names its object.
- Work: a spread. The plate on the left, the object large, the line it stands
  for under it; the entry on the right, with the full museum label. A work with
  no object shows its Greek title on the wall.
- Reader: no picture. The wall becomes the head bar; Greek and English side by
  side at both widths, each with its own Bekker numbers.
- Search and lexicon: no picture. The word set large, labelled like an object.

**Type and colour.** GFS Didot for titles and all Greek (the Greek Font Society’s Didot); Source Serif 4 for English; Fira Sans for
labels. Near-white paper, a deep blue-black wall with a pool of light, one gilt
accent; in dark mode the paper darkens too.

**Images and rights.** 26 objects: 23 from The Met (public domain), 3 from the
Cleveland Museum of Art (CC0); the portrait is a public-domain photograph on
Wikimedia Commons. Each is cut out once (BiRefNet, MIT licence) and serves both
widths. Credits sit in each label and in `sources.md`, with the rejects.

**The risk.** GFS Didot’s Greek may not please John either. The 15 works with
no object get a title panel, calmer than the object pages. A cut-out stands or
falls by its edge, so each new object needs the same care.
