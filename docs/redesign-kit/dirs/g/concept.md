# G · Sentence

**The idea.** Each page opens on one sentence of Aristotle's, set in Greek as large as the page allows, with every word glossed small in red beneath it. The English follows in italic; there are no pictures, so the type is the image.

**Why it suits Aristotle.** He is read sentence by sentence, and many of his sentences are famous. A gloss under each word is what a reader of Greek writes in the margin, and what this site does when you click a word. Lowercase Greek keeps the accents and breathings that capitals drop, so the marks become part of the picture.

**Pages.**
- Home: the Metaphysics' first sentence fills the first screen; "Another sentence" steps through twenty works. The works follow by subject as run-on lists, English and Greek titles together.
- Work: the title in large red italic, then the work's own sentence. The Ethics sets its four translations of the swallow line one under another, each one's word for ἔαρ in red: "spring", "summer". Books fold shut; chapters lead.
- Reader: Book I opens on its first sentence, glossed. Chapters start with a large figure and Ostwald's title. The text is plain, Greek and English side by side.
- Search: the hits as a concordance, every form in one red column.
- Lexicon: the headword, then the sentence that defines the human good, κατ' ἀρετήν in red.

**Type and colour.** Noto Serif Display, a Didone with full polytonic Greek: black and condensed for Greek sentences, light for figures and headwords, italic for titles and English. Libertinus Serif, a classicist's face, for reading and glosses. Black and one vermilion on white; dark mode sets light type on warm black.

**Images.** None. Instead, forty quotations, one per work (three works set their Greek title). Each Greek line is checked against the site's sources or Bekker's text, each English line against the site's translation files, each with its Bekker number.

**The risk.** Big black type can shout, and every gloss must be right.
