But let them be indefinite verbs, because they are similarly inherent,
both in whatever exists, and whatever does not exist. In like manner,
I do not call, *was well*, and *will be well*, verbs, but cases of a verb. But
they differ from a verb; because the one, indeed, (i. e. a verb) signifies
in addition to something else the present time; but the others, that
which is about the present time. Verbs, therefore, so called by them-
selves, are nouns[^3], and signify something; (for he who pronounces
[some verb] stops the discursive power [of the hearer[^4],] and he who hears
acquiesces,) but they do not yet signify whether a thing is or is not[^5].

Thus runs, signifies the race which now is; did run, signifies the race which was before. But
when Aristotle says, “ of which no part is significant,” this difference distinguishes a verb from
a sentence. And when he says, that “ it is always an indication of those things which are as-

signified by a verb with another thing. This additional signification, however, of connection,
cannot be sufficiently understood without a sentence, as the philosopher says at the end of this
chapter. When, therefore, we say the man runs, or the man is running, we conjoin the attri-
bute with the subject, viz. the race with man, which power of conjoining is in the verb runs,
and in the verbal copula *is*.
¶[^9] Universals are predicated of a subject; accidents are in a subject. But that which is predi-
cated of another thing, is either an universal, or an accident.
¶[^1] That is, they are always an indication of an attribute which is predicated of a subject.
¶[^2] That is, to the words *is not well, is not ill*, which are different from verbs.
[^3] Aristotle now assumes the word *noun* in a more extended sense than in the former chapter;
viz. he assumes it for every significant word.
[^4] That is, he causes the hearer to conceive in his mind, the thing signified by the verb.
[^5] That is, before they are assumed in enunciation, and conjoined with nouns.
